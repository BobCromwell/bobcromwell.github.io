Expensive Models vs. Cheap Models

2026-06-24 · 2 min · 253 words · bob

The Old Wisdom#

There used to be a prevailing view: “Always use the most expensive model.” Anthropic’s Opus, for example.

The reasoning was straightforward: the most expensive model is the smartest model.

For any given task, the most expensive model gets it right in one shot. A cheaper model might get it wrong, and after a few rounds of correction, you’ve spent more in time and tokens than if you’d just used the expensive one from the start.

The trade-offs of using the most expensive model were equally clear:

  1. Expensive
  2. Slow

Recent Experience#

But lately, using DeepSeekV4Flash has made me rethink this. A lot of simple tasks—tweaking Emacs config, writing a short Python script, things like that—get done just fine. And they’re fast and cheap.

A Better Model#

Even for fully automated tasks — no human in the loop — the requirements are the same:

  1. Correct
  2. Cheap
  3. Fast

What’s changed is that cheap models have crossed a new baseline in capability. They’re good enough for a growing range of “simple enough” work.

That said, when I tried using DeepSeekV4Flash to modify OpenClaw’s configuration, it made two mistakes. In that case, I’d have been better off just using GPT-5.5 from the start.

This suggests a useful skill: being able to judge the difficulty of a task before you start. There’s growing consensus around some straightforward categories:

  1. (Simple) translation
  2. Summarization

Over time, more categories will solidify.

Conclusion#

There’s room for both cheap and expensive models. The trick is knowing which bucket your task falls into.
