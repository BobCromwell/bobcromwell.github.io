Cheap Models

2026-06-26 · 2 min · 289 words · 田宝明 | Translations:
  • Zh

There’s a familiar refrain: “always use the most expensive model.” Anthropic’s Opus 4.6, the top of the pricing pyramid.

The reasoning goes: the most expensive model is the smartest model. For any given task, the premium model nails it on the first try. A cheap model might get it wrong, require corrections, and by the time you’re done, the cumulative time and token cost has surpassed what you’d have paid for Opus upfront.

What the expensive model buys you comes at a cost though:

  1. Expensive
  2. Slow

A New Baseline

Recently I’ve been using DeepSeekV4Flash, and it’s made me rethink this. For plenty of simple tasks—tweaking Emacs config, writing throwaway Python scripts—it just works. Fast. Cheap.

Even for tasks that don’t need a human in the loop, the requirements are the same:

  1. Correct
  2. Cheap
  3. Fast

The cheap model’s capability has simply caught up to a new baseline.

But Then It Fails

Of course, the story doesn’t end there. When I used DeepSeekV4Flash to modify an OpenClaw configuration, it got it wrong. Twice. And in that moment it felt obvious: should’ve used GPT-5.5 from the start.

The Real Question

So the ability to judge task difficulty before starting would be genuinely valuable. What we currently agree are simple tasks:

  1. (Straightforward) translation
  2. Summarization
  3. Text formatting

But “simple” is slippery. Editing Emacs config looks trivial until the model doesn’t know about recent built-in changes. Translation seems easy until you hit idioms that require real cultural understanding.

The real insight isn’t that cheap models are bad, or that expensive models are always worth it. It’s that the baseline keeps shifting. Cheap models today are capable enough for a widening circle of tasks—and knowing where that circle ends is what separates good judgment from superstition.
